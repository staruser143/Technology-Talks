**Scenario**

An insurance company's fraud-flagging system uses escalation-based review: claims where the model's confidence score is above 90% (either "clearly fraudulent" or "clearly legitimate") are handled automatically, and claims below that threshold are routed to human fraud investigators. The design was based on the assumption that confidence score reliably tracks correctness — high confidence should mean the system is usually right, low confidence should mean it's genuinely uncertain.

A retrospective audit six months in finds a concerning pattern: a meaningful number of claims the system marked "95% confident: legitimate" turned out, on later investigation (triggered by unrelated red flags), to actually be fraudulent — the system wasn't just wrong on these cases, it was *confidently* wrong, meaning they never got routed to a human investigator at all, since they cleared the 90% threshold for automatic handling. Meanwhile, investigators report that a large share of what does get escalated to them consists of claims where the system's confidence score sits just below 90% but the case itself is actually quite straightforward — investigators say they can resolve most escalated cases in under a minute because the correct answer is obvious to a human immediately.

**Question**: What's the core issue with this escalation-based design, and what should the team do?


**Question**: What's the core issue with this escalation-based design, and what should the team do?

A) The escalation-based mode itself was the wrong choice; the team should switch to pre-action approval on every claim regardless of confidence, since escalation-based review has now been shown to be fundamentally unreliable.

B) The core issue is that the escalation threshold assumed the model's **confidence score reliably correlates with actual correctness**, but the audit reveals this assumption doesn't hold — the system is sometimes confidently wrong (high-confidence "legitimate" claims that were actually fraudulent, never reaching a human) while also routing some genuinely easy, low-effort cases to humans just because they happened to sit slightly below the threshold. Escalation-based review is only as good as the confidence signal it routes on; if that signal is miscalibrated, the routing itself is unreliable regardless of how sound the underlying HITL mode is conceptually. The team should validate and calibrate the confidence score against actual outcomes (does 95% confidence really correspond to ~95% real-world accuracy on a held-out, verified sample) before trusting it as a routing mechanism, and consider supplementing it with independent risk signals (not solely the model's self-reported confidence) for determining what gets escalated.

C) The issue is that the 90% threshold is too low; simply raising it to 99% would fully resolve both problems described.

D) The issue is unrelated to the escalation design; investigators are simply too fast at resolving cases, and the fix is to require investigators to spend a minimum amount of time on every escalated case regardless of how quickly they reach a conclusion.



Correct — and this scenario exposes a failure mode specific to escalation-based review that didn't come up when you correctly selected it for Workflow 3: the mode itself can be exactly right in concept while still failing in practice, because it silently depends on an assumption (confidence tracks correctness) that has to be independently verified, not just trusted because it sounds reasonable.

**Why B is right**

Escalation-based review only works if the thing you're routing on — here, the model's confidence score — actually does what you're relying on it to do: separate genuinely easy cases from genuinely hard ones. The audit reveals this assumption breaks down in *both* directions simultaneously, which is what makes this scenario a clean test of the underlying mechanism rather than just one symptom:

- **Confidently wrong cases never reaching a human**: this is the more dangerous failure. A claim scored "95% confident: legitimate" that's actually fraudulent isn't just an error — it's an error the system has no mechanism to catch, precisely because high confidence is what exempts a case from human review in this design. If confidence doesn't reliably track correctness, then the threshold isn't actually separating "safe to automate" from "needs a human" — it's separating "the model sounds sure" from "the model sounds unsure," and those aren't necessarily the same thing at all. This is directly the "trust a score without checking what it's actually measuring" lesson from your entire Domain 4 metric-selection arc, now applied to a *routing* decision instead of an evaluation report — the consequence here is arguably worse, because a miscalibrated eval metric misleads a report, while a miscalibrated confidence threshold actively lets bad decisions bypass the safeguard meant to catch them.

- **Easy cases wasting investigator time**: the mirror problem — cases sitting just below the threshold that a human resolves "in under a minute" suggest the confidence score isn't well-calibrated on the *low* end either; some cases scoring under 90% aren't actually ambiguous, they're just cases the model happens to express lower confidence on for reasons that don't track real difficulty. This wastes the exact resource (human investigator attention) that escalation-based review exists to conserve and direct efficiently.

Both problems trace to the same root cause: the routing signal (confidence) was assumed to be reliable without being independently validated against real outcomes. The fix, correctly stated in B, is to check that assumption directly — take a held-out, verified sample and confirm that "95% confidence" claims really do resolve correctly around 95% of the time (this is a calibration check, conceptually similar to the inter-annotator-agreement and eval-staleness verification instincts from Domain 4, just applied to a live routing mechanism) — and consider supplementing self-reported model confidence with independent risk signals (claim size, claimant history, anomaly indicators) rather than relying on the model's own confidence estimate as the sole arbiter of what counts as "clear-cut."

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** overcorrects by abandoning the entire HITL mode based on a calibration failure specific to *this implementation* — but escalation-based review was correctly identified as the right conceptual fit for high-volume, variable-difficulty workflows in your last scenario, and nothing here suggests that judgment was wrong. The problem isn't "escalation-based review is unreliable as a category," it's "this particular confidence signal wasn't properly validated before being trusted to drive escalation" — a calibration fix, not a mode-abandonment situation. Switching to pre-action approval on every claim would also reintroduce the exact scalability problem escalation-based review was chosen to solve in the first place.
- **C)** proposes a blunt numeric adjustment without addressing the actual mechanism — raising the threshold to 99% doesn't fix miscalibration, it just shifts where the (still-uncalibrated) boundary sits. If confidence scores don't reliably track correctness, a higher cutoff could still let confidently-wrong cases through (just fewer of them, or different ones), and doesn't address why some sub-90% cases are trivially easy — that's a calibration problem, not a threshold-height problem, the same category error as your earlier "just lower the expense threshold" wrong answer.
- **D)** blames the investigators' efficiency rather than the routing mechanism that sent them cases not worth their time in the first place — forcing artificial delay on cases that are genuinely, correctly easy doesn't improve anything; it just wastes investigator time on purpose instead of by accident, without addressing the real problem, which is that the routing signal is failing to distinguish easy from hard cases reliably in either direction.

**Pattern to remember, extending the HITL arc**: choosing the right HITL *mode* (pre-action, post-action, escalation-based) is necessary but not sufficient — escalation-based review specifically introduces a new dependency (the routing signal's reliability) that has to be independently validated, not assumed. A model's self-reported confidence is a plausible-sounding routing signal, but "plausible-sounding" and "actually calibrated against real outcomes" are different properties, and the gap between them is exactly where this scenario's failure lived.
