Moving to sub-topic 2: identifying risks, limitations, and failure modes of LLM systems.

**Scenario**

A legal-tech company deploys a Claude-powered contract-drafting assistant for their internal legal team. The team is confident in the tool because "lawyers review everything before it goes out, so any AI mistakes get caught." Six months in, an internal audit finds that draft contracts increasingly go out with only minimal review — junior lawyers report they've started treating the assistant's first draft as "basically final," skimming rather than scrutinizing clauses, because the tool has been reliable in the past and reviewing every clause carefully feels redundant given how good recent drafts have been. One contract went out with a subtly incorrect liability clause that a careful review would have caught, but didn't, because the reviewing lawyer's attention had shifted from "verify this is correct" to "quickly confirm nothing looks obviously wrong."

**Question**: Which LLM system risk/failure mode does this scenario primarily illustrate, and what does it imply for how human-in-the-loop safeguards should be designed?

A) This illustrates a hallucination risk specifically — the liability clause error is a fabrication, and the fix is a groundedness check on every clause against a legal reference database, unrelated to how human review is structured.

B) This illustrates **overreliance** — a known LLM system risk where users' trust in a generally-reliable system erodes their own scrutiny over time, degrading the effectiveness of a human-in-the-loop safeguard that depends on genuine, careful human attention to function as intended. A human-in-the-loop control that assumes reviewers will always scrutinize output as carefully as they did on day one is fragile; the design should account for attention decay (e.g., structured review checklists forcing engagement with specific clause types, periodic calibration exercises, or spot-check audits of "reviewed" output) rather than assuming the presence of a human reviewer alone guarantees the safeguard still works as originally intended.

C) This is not a real risk category — human error is outside the scope of LLM system risk analysis, which should focus exclusively on model behavior, not reviewer behavior.

D) This illustrates a data leakage risk, since the liability clause represents confidential information exposed inappropriately.


Correct — and this scenario names a genuinely distinct LLM-system risk category from everything covered in the guardrails arc: not a flaw in the *system's* design, but a flaw in how the human safeguard *around* the system degrades over time as a predictable side effect of the system working well.

**Why B is right**

Trace the actual causal chain in the scenario: the tool didn't get worse, and nothing about its output changed to trigger less scrutiny — the *reviewers* changed, precisely because the tool had been reliable. This is the specific mechanism of overreliance: sustained good performance from an AI system erodes the vigilance of the humans meant to check it, not through any malicious or careless intent, but as a natural human response to a tool that "usually gets it right." Junior lawyers didn't decide to stop caring — their attention genuinely shifted from active verification to passive confirmation ("quickly confirm nothing looks obviously wrong") because careful, clause-by-clause scrutiny started to feel redundant against a track record of reliability.

This is a critical, distinct risk to name separately from every guardrail-design lesson in the prior arc, because it exposes something those scenarios didn't cover: **a human-in-the-loop safeguard's effectiveness isn't fixed once a human reviewer is placed in the loop — it can silently degrade over time, even with the exact same review step nominally still in place.** The salary-change scenario taught you "add a human checkpoint for consequential actions." This scenario teaches the necessary follow-up: a human checkpoint isn't a permanent, self-sustaining guarantee — it needs to be designed with the expectation that reviewer attention will erode as trust builds, the same way you learned that compaction can't be trusted to preserve critical facts indefinitely, or that a metric optimized against long enough can diverge from its original purpose (your satisfaction-score scenario). The fix, correctly named in B, is designing the review process to actively resist this decay — structured checklists forcing engagement with specific risk-prone clause types (rather than open-ended "look it over"), periodic calibration exercises, or independent spot-check audits verifying that "reviewed" contracts actually received genuine scrutiny, not just a rubber stamp.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** misidentifies the risk category by focusing on the *content* of the error (a subtly incorrect clause) rather than the *process failure* that let it through. Whether the liability clause was a hallucination, a genuine drafting weakness, or simply a plausible-sounding error the model itself was uncertain about is actually secondary to the scenario's real point — the entire scenario is built around explaining *why the review process failed to catch it*, not diagnosing what specifically was wrong with the clause. A groundedness check might be a reasonable additional safeguard, but it doesn't address the root cause described: eroded reviewer scrutiny, which would eventually let *some* category of error slip through regardless of what technical content-layer fix is added upstream.
- **C)** draws an artificial boundary excluding human-reviewer behavior from LLM system risk analysis — but this directly contradicts the entire purpose of a human-in-the-loop safeguard, which only functions as a risk mitigation if the human's behavior within it is accounted for. A risk framework that only analyzes model outputs and ignores how humans actually interact with those outputs over time misses one of the most well-documented, practically important risk categories in real-world AI deployment — overreliance is specifically a risk that emerges from the human-AI interaction, not from the model in isolation.
- **D)** reaches for an unrelated risk category with no support in the scenario — nothing describes confidential information being inappropriately exposed to an unauthorized party; the liability clause error is a drafting/accuracy problem that went undetected, not a data leakage event (which would involve information disclosure, not a factual/legal error within a document already intended for the client).

**Pattern to remember, opening this new risk-identification arc**: overreliance is a risk that specifically targets the *safeguard itself*, not the system being safeguarded — and it's counterintuitive precisely because it's caused by the system performing *well*, not poorly. A human-in-the-loop control designed assuming constant, day-one-level scrutiny is fragile against exactly the success that makes the underlying system worth deploying in the first place; robust designs need to actively account for and counteract this erosion, not just assume a human's presence in the workflow is a permanent, self-maintaining guarantee.
