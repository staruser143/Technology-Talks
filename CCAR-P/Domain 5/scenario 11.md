**Why B is right**

The team's justification — "broader access now avoids future integration work" — is word-for-word the same scope-creep reasoning you already correctly rejected in your CRM scenario ("we might add features later that need it"). The mechanism is identical: granting access based on hypothetical future need rather than the actual, current task's requirements. What makes this scenario distinct, and worth its own dedicated treatment under this sub-topic, is that HIPAA's **minimum necessary standard** turns that same reasoning flaw into a specific, named regulatory violation rather than just a general security best-practice lapse. A discharge summary for the current admission needs the current admission's clinical data — vitals, treatment notes, medications, discharge instructions. It does not need decades of unrelated historical records, and it especially doesn't need mental health or substance abuse treatment history, categories the scenario correctly flags as "often carry even stricter protections than general PHI" (in the US, substance-use treatment records specifically have historically been subject to even tighter federal restrictions than general medical records, requiring more explicit authorization for disclosure).

The fix mirrors your auth/authz scope-minimization instinct exactly — scope access to what the specific task genuinely requires, not what might conceivably be useful someday — but the framing here matters for the exam: this isn't just "good practice," it's compliance with a named regulatory requirement, meaning the cost of getting it wrong isn't just increased security risk in the abstract, it's direct regulatory exposure for the hospital as a HIPAA-covered entity.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** treats "operating within an authorized system, on behalf of authorized staff" as sufficient justification for unlimited access, but this misunderstands what minimum necessary actually requires — being a legitimate, authorized user or system doesn't exempt you from the requirement to access only what a specific task needs; the hospital being HIPAA-covered doesn't mean any internal use of PHI is automatically compliant, it means the hospital is obligated to apply minimum-necessary principles to every internal use, including this one.
- **C)** draws an artificial line, treating only the specially-protected categories (mental health, substance abuse) as the actual concern while giving general historical records a pass — but minimum necessary applies to *all* PHI, not just the most sensitive subcategories. Decades-old, unrelated admission records are still PHI the current task doesn't need; the specially-protected categories make the violation more severe, but they don't define its boundary — the general historical access is also a real violation on its own.
- **D)** recategorizes a regulatory compliance issue as a pure infrastructure/cost concern, missing the point entirely — the problem isn't that broader access costs more to store or maintain, it's that accessing PHI beyond what a specific task requires is itself the violation, regardless of storage cost. This is the same category error as treating a consequential-action guardrail gap as merely a UX inconvenience.
