Moving to the transparency dimension: whether users understand they're interacting with an AI system, and whether the system's reasoning, limitations, and confidence are appropriately disclosed rather than presented as more authoritative or certain than warranted.

**Scenario**

A telehealth company deploys a Claude-powered chat feature that answers patients' general health questions before they see a doctor. The interface is designed to feel like a seamless extension of the clinical experience — it's styled identically to messages from the patient's actual care team, uses a friendly first-person tone ("I'd recommend monitoring that for a few more days"), and nowhere on the screen, in the onboarding flow, or in the conversation itself does it disclose that the patient is talking to an AI system rather than a human clinician or staff member. Internal user research finds that a significant share of patients using the feature believe they're communicating with a real member of their care team.

**Question**: What's the core issue here, and why does it matter beyond just being a stylistic choice?


**Question**: What's the core issue here, and why does it matter beyond just being a stylistic choice?

A) There's no real issue — as long as the health information provided is accurate, whether patients know they're talking to an AI versus a human is purely a matter of UX preference with no substantive consequence.

B) The core issue is a **transparency failure**: patients have a right to know they're interacting with an AI system, particularly in a healthcare context where that knowledge materially affects how much weight, trust, and follow-up scrutiny they should reasonably apply to what they're told. A patient who believes they're talking to their actual care team is likely to trust and act on the AI's responses with the same confidence they'd give a clinician — including in situations where the AI's limitations (it can't examine them, doesn't have full clinical judgment, may be wrong) should warrant more caution, not less. This isn't a stylistic issue — it directly affects patient safety decisions (e.g., whether to seek in-person care sooner) made based on a false belief about the source's authority. The fix is clear, unavoidable disclosure that the patient is interacting with an AI system, both at first use and ideally reinforced within the conversation itself, not just an easy-to-miss line in a policy document.

C) The issue only matters for legal liability purposes; since the company can update its terms of service to technically disclose AI use in the fine print, no interface-level change is actually necessary as long as legal documentation exists somewhere.

D) The issue is overreliance, identical to the legal-drafting scenario from earlier in this domain, and should be addressed the same way — by improving the human review process rather than anything related to the interface itself.

Correct — and this scenario names transparency as a genuinely distinct risk from everything else in this domain's arc, worth being precise about how it differs even from the adjacent concepts it might get confused with.

**Why B is right**

The core mechanism here connects directly to your overreliance scenario, but it's actually a *different* failure with a *different* fix, which is exactly why B is careful to distinguish it. In your legal-drafting overreliance scenario, reviewers *knew* they were checking AI output and their scrutiny eroded over time because the tool had proven reliable — a trust-calibration problem that develops through experience. Here, the problem is upstream of that entirely: patients never had the *opportunity* to calibrate their trust appropriately, because they don't know they're evaluating AI output at all — they believe they're receiving guidance from their actual care team, and apply the trust level appropriate to that belief from the very first interaction. That's not gradual erosion of appropriate scrutiny; it's the complete absence of the basic information a person needs to apply *any* appropriate scrutiny in the first place.

Why this matters specifically in a healthcare context, as B states: the AI's real limitations — it can't physically examine the patient, doesn't have the full clinical picture, can be wrong — are exactly the kind of limitations a patient should weigh when deciding how much to trust a given piece of guidance and whether to seek further care. A patient who believes "my care team said to just monitor this for a few more days" will very reasonably behave differently than one who knows "an AI chatbot said to monitor this" — the second framing appropriately invites more independent judgment about whether to seek care sooner regardless of what the message said. Withholding that basic fact isn't a cosmetic UX choice; it removes the patient's ability to make an appropriately-calibrated decision about their own health, which is precisely the harm transparency requirements exist to prevent.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** treats accuracy as the only thing that matters, but transparency isn't primarily about whether the *content* is correct — it's about whether the *user* has the information needed to appropriately weigh that content, regardless of its accuracy. Even perfectly accurate AI-generated health guidance still warrants different scrutiny and follow-up behavior from a patient than the same content attributed to their actual doctor, because the AI's limitations (no physical exam, no full clinical context, no professional accountability in the same sense) are real regardless of how often it happens to be right.
- **C)** repeats a version of the "instructions/documentation buried somewhere technically satisfies the requirement" mistake seen elsewhere in this domain — a disclosure that exists only in fine-print terms of service that essentially no user reads doesn't achieve what transparency is actually meant to accomplish (a user genuinely understanding what they're interacting with, in the moment it matters). This is the compliance equivalent of a guardrail that's technically present but functionally invisible.
- **D)** conflates two genuinely distinct concepts. Overreliance describes erosion of scrutiny *by someone who knows they're evaluating AI output* over repeated exposure to good performance. This scenario describes patients who never had the baseline information to apply appropriate scrutiny *at all*, from the very first interaction — there's no "review process" to improve here in the way there was for the lawyers, because the patients aren't in a review role at all; they're the direct recipients of guidance they don't know is AI-generated. The fix accordingly is completely different: overreliance needs structural safeguards against attention decay in a review process; this needs upfront, unavoidable disclosure of what the patient is actually interacting with.

**Pattern to remember, closing the transparency dimension**: transparency failures and overreliance are related but distinct — transparency is about whether someone has the basic information needed to calibrate trust *at all*, while overreliance is about trust calibration eroding *despite* having that information, through repeated positive experience. A system can fail on either dimension independently, and confusing them (as option D does) leads to applying the wrong category of fix — better review-process design doesn't help someone who was never told what they were reviewing in the first place.
