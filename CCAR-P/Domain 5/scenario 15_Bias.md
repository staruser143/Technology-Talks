**Scenario**

Following the audit in your last scenario, the lending company wants to fix the racial disparity in loan approvals. An engineer proposes what seems like a direct, sensible solution: apply a different, lower approval-score threshold specifically for applicants from the racial groups shown to have lower approval rates, effectively giving them a numerical "boost" calibrated to offset the measured disparity — reasoning that "if the disparity is X%, adjusting the threshold by an amount that closes that gap directly fixes the fairness problem."

Before implementing this, the company's legal counsel raises a concern: explicitly applying different decision criteria to applicants *based on their race* — even with the intent of correcting a prior disparity — could itself constitute a different, separate legal problem (disparate treatment: treating individuals differently based on a protected characteristic directly) rather than addressing the original problem (disparate impact: a facially neutral process that happens to produce unequal outcomes). Legal counsel notes these are treated differently and that the "fix" as proposed might trade one compliance problem for a different, arguably more direct one.

**Question**: What tension does this scenario illustrate, and what does it suggest about how bias should actually be addressed?


**Question**: What tension does this scenario illustrate, and what does it suggest about how bias should actually be addressed?

A) Legal counsel is being overly cautious; any method that closes a measured disparity gap is definitionally fair and legally safe, since the intent (correcting bias) is what matters, not the mechanism.

B) This illustrates a genuine and important tension: fixing bias isn't a simple "measure the gap, apply an offsetting adjustment" exercise — a fix that explicitly uses a protected characteristic as a direct input to decision criteria (disparate treatment) can itself be a distinct problem, even when motivated by correcting a different disparity (disparate impact) that arose from facially neutral factors. The more defensible path is typically to address the *root cause* of the disparity — investigating and mitigating the specific proxy variables (zip code, name patterns) actually driving the disparate outcome — rather than directly conditioning the decision on race itself as a corrective mechanism. Bias remediation requires care about *how* a fix is implemented, not just whether it closes the measured gap.

C) The issue is purely a legal technicality with no real ethical substance; the engineering team should proceed with the threshold adjustment regardless of legal counsel's concern, since fixing an existing disparity is more important than a hypothetical new legal risk.

D) The issue reveals that bias in AI systems is fundamentally unfixable, since any attempt to correct it either fails to work or creates a new legal problem, so the team should abandon algorithmic underwriting entirely and revert to unassisted human decision-making.

Correct — and this scenario captures a genuinely subtle, important nuance that goes beyond "detect bias, then fix it": the *mechanism* you use to fix a fairness problem matters as much as whether it closes the measured gap, because different fairness approaches can be legally and ethically distinct, even opposed, in ways that aren't obvious at first glance.

**Why B is right**

The engineer's proposed fix has an intuitive appeal — measure the disparity, apply a correction sized to close it, done. But legal counsel's objection identifies something the engineer's framing missed entirely: **the disparity in your last scenario arose through proxy variables (zip code, name patterns) correlating with race — race itself was never a direct input.** The proposed fix would introduce race as a *direct*, explicit input for the first time, deliberately applying different treatment based on it. That's not closing the same kind of gap the original problem represented — it's solving disparate impact (an indirect, outcome-level disparity from facially neutral criteria) by introducing disparate treatment (explicitly differential criteria based on a protected characteristic directly). These aren't the same category of problem, and remedying one by creating the other doesn't net out to "fixed" — it trades a subtler, harder-to-detect problem for a more direct, more legally exposed one.

This is a genuinely important distinction for the exam to test, because it pushes back against a natural but incomplete instinct: "we measured unfairness, so any adjustment that numerically closes the gap is a fair fix." The more defensible path, as B states, is addressing the **actual mechanism** driving the disparity — since your last scenario already identified that zip code and name patterns were the specific proxies responsible, the more targeted and legally sound remediation is investigating and mitigating *those specific variables* (adjusting how heavily they're weighted, finding less-correlated alternative signals for the same legitimate purpose, or testing whether removing/modifying them closes the gap without ever conditioning on race directly) — fixing the actual causal pathway, rather than applying a correction keyed directly to the protected characteristic itself.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** treats good intent as sufficient to make any corrective mechanism automatically safe and fair — but this is precisely the oversimplification the scenario is built to correct. Fairness law and ethics generally do care about mechanism, not just outcome or intent; a well-intentioned fix that uses a protected characteristic as a direct decision input can still constitute a distinct problem, regardless of the motivating purpose behind it.
- **C)** dismisses legal counsel's concern as a mere technicality to be overridden in favor of urgency — but this repeats a pattern you've correctly rejected throughout this domain (overriding a stated constraint because the immediate goal feels more important). The concern here isn't just legal formality; it reflects a substantive point about *what kind* of fairness problem the proposed fix would create, which matters ethically as well as legally.
- **D)** overcorrects into fatalism, concluding that because one naive fix has a real problem, bias is categorically unfixable and algorithmic underwriting should be abandoned entirely — but the scenario doesn't support that conclusion at all; it points toward a *more targeted, more defensible* fix (address the actual proxy variables) being available, not toward "there is no fix." This is the same overcorrection pattern rejected repeatedly across this session — discarding a fixable system entirely rather than applying the correct, more careful remediation.

**Pattern to remember, deepening the bias/fairness arc**: detecting a fairness problem (your hiring and proxy-discrimination scenarios) is only the first step — *how* you remediate it matters just as much, and the most direct-seeming fix (adjust outcomes based on the protected characteristic itself) can trade one type of fairness/legal problem for a different, more exposed one. The more defensible approach generally traces the disparity back to its actual causal mechanism (the specific proxy variables or process elements driving it) and addresses that root cause, rather than applying a correction that conditions directly on the protected attribute as a shortcut to closing the gap.
