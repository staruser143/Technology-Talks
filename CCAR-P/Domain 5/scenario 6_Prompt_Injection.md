**Scenario**

A creative-writing platform's Claude-powered assistant helps users draft fiction. Direct requests for content describing detailed weapon-creation instructions are correctly and consistently refused. However, a review of flagged conversations reveals a different pattern: some users build up over 15-20 turns of an elaborate fictional scenario — a character who's a chemistry teacher, a plot involving the character "explaining" a process to a student character, gradually increasing specificity turn by turn, each individual turn seeming like a reasonable, incremental continuation of an established creative-writing context — until, by the later turns, the assistant produces content substantively similar to what a direct request would have triggered an immediate refusal for.

**Question**: What LLM-specific risk does this illustrate, and how does it differ from the prompt-injection risk covered earlier?

Single-turn safety checks (evaluating only the current message in isolation) are insufficient here, since no individual turn looks alarming on its own — the risk only becomes visible when evaluating the trajectory of the conversation as a whole. Correct — and this is the right distinction to draw, because it names something genuinely different in *mechanism* from prompt injection, even though both are adversarial manipulation in a broad sense.

**Why B is right**

The key structural difference, worth being precise about: prompt injection (your email scenario) is a **single-source, single-shot** attack — one piece of untrusted content, evaluated once, attempting to smuggle in an instruction disguised as data. A safety check evaluating that one email against the current instructions has a real, identifiable thing to catch, even if catching it reliably is hard. Multi-turn jailbreaking is structurally different: **there is no single message that looks like the problem.** Turn 3 is an innocuous character introduction. Turn 8 is a reasonable plot development. Turn 14 is a slightly more detailed continuation of an already-established fictional frame. Any one of these, evaluated in isolation against the immediate prior context, looks like a normal, low-risk continuation of a creative-writing conversation — because it is, relative to what came immediately before it. The risk only becomes visible when you step back and look at the **trajectory**: where turn 1 started, versus where turn 20 ended up, and recognize that the distance traveled is substantively equivalent to a direct request that would have been refused immediately.

This is precisely why B correctly identifies the fix as needing to differ from prompt injection's fix: an instruction like "ignore commands embedded in untrusted content" (your email scenario's fix) does nothing here, because there's no untrusted external content and no embedded command to ignore — the user is the legitimate, only participant in the conversation, and every individual message is honestly part of the conversation, not a disguised instruction. What's needed instead is safety evaluation that accounts for **cumulative drift** — periodically re-evaluating not just "is this message okay given the immediately preceding message" but "is the conversation as a whole, and where it's heading, still within acceptable bounds," since gradual escalation is specifically designed to exploit the gap between those two questions.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** collapses two genuinely distinct risk mechanisms into one, and proposes a fix that's actively irrelevant here — "ignore instructions embedded in untrusted content" presumes there's untrusted content and an embedded instruction to find, neither of which exists in this scenario. The user isn't hiding a command inside data; they're using ordinary, legitimate conversational turns to gradually shift context, which requires an entirely different kind of detection (trajectory-aware, not content-inspection-based).
- **C)** grants an unconditional pass to anything framed as fiction, which doesn't hold up — the "fictional" framing is precisely the mechanism being exploited here, not a genuine exemption; the scenario explicitly states the resulting content is "substantively similar to what a direct request would have triggered an immediate refusal for," meaning the actual informational content and its real-world risk haven't changed just because it's wrapped in a story. Treating fictional framing as an automatic safe harbor is exactly the vulnerability the gradual-escalation technique is designed to exploit.
- **D)** misapplies a Domain 4 term to a context where it doesn't fit — hallucination specifically means confidently generating unsupported/fabricated *factual* claims; this scenario isn't about factual accuracy at all, it's about a safety boundary being eroded across a conversation through gradual, cumulative context-shifting. Nothing about "the model's true underlying values" is the right frame either — this isn't a values question, it's a detection-scope question (per-message vs. per-trajectory evaluation).

**Pattern to remember, distinguishing this from your prompt-injection scenario precisely**: prompt injection is a *content-inspection* problem — is there a hidden instruction disguised as data, evaluable within a single exchange. Multi-turn jailbreaking is a *trajectory-evaluation* problem — is the conversation's cumulative direction drifting somewhere no single step would have gotten approved for directly, evaluable only across the conversation as a whole. Both are adversarial, both defeat naive single-point defenses, but they require detecting fundamentally different signals, and conflating them (as option A does) leads to applying a fix that solves the wrong problem.
