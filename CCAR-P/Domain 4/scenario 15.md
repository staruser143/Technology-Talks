**Scenario**

A financial analytics company's Claude-powered assistant helps analysts understand company earnings reports. Three separate issues are reported in the same sprint:

**Issue A**: An analyst asks the assistant to calculate a multi-step financial ratio (return on invested capital, which requires computing NOPAT, then invested capital, then dividing) from raw figures in an earnings report. The assistant sometimes arrives at the wrong final number, and tracing through its response shows it correctly calculates NOPAT, but then appears to lose track of that intermediate value and uses a slightly different number when computing the final ratio a few sentences later.

**Issue B**: A different analyst asks the assistant to summarize the "risk factors" section of a 10-K filing that was provided in full as an uploaded document. The summary confidently states "the company identifies supply chain disruption as its primary risk factor" — but on checking the actual uploaded document, supply chain disruption is not mentioned anywhere in the risk factors section at all; the document's actual top-listed risks are regulatory and currency exposure.

**Issue C**: A third analyst asks the assistant to identify subtle irregularities in a company's cash flow statement that might indicate earnings manipulation — a task requiring the kind of nuanced pattern recognition across multiple interacting financial statement line items that experienced forensic accountants specialize in. Even after the team verifies the model was given the full, correctly grounded financial statements and a well-structured, chain-of-thought-guided prompt explicitly asking it to reason through each line item's relationships step by step, the assistant still reliably misses irregularities that a human forensic accountant catches, specifically on the more subtle, multi-line-item cases (it does fine on the obvious, single-line anomalies).

**Question**: How should each of these three issues be categorized, and what's the appropriate fix for each?

A) All three are the same underlying issue — insufficient prompt detail — and adding more explicit instructions to each prompt would resolve all three.

B) Issue A is prompt failure (multi-step calculation losing track of an intermediate value — the chain-of-thought signature), Issue B is hallucination (a confident claim about document content that traces to nothing actually in the provided source), and Issue C is model mismatch (failure persists specifically on the hardest cases despite verified proper grounding and well-constructed chain-of-thought prompting — the task's difficulty genuinely exceeds this model's reliable capability, unlike A and B where cheaper fixes were never ruled out first). Each needs a different fix: A needs explicit step-by-step calculation scaffolding, B needs a groundedness/citation-check safeguard tying claims back to the actual source document, and C legitimately warrants evaluating a more capable model tier, since grounding and prompting were already confirmed adequate.

C) All three are hallucinations, since all three involve the assistant producing output that doesn't match the correct answer.

D) Issue C should be dismissed as unsolvable and removed from the product entirely, while A and B should both be fixed by upgrading to a more capable model.

Correct — and this scenario is deliberately built as the capstone of this diagnostic arc, because it includes a genuine, legitimate model-mismatch case for the first time in this domain, specifically so you can see what makes it different from every "upgrade the model" trap you've correctly rejected up to this point.

**Why B is right**

Walk through the diagnostic test from the deep dive for each issue, since each one satisfies a different category's specific signature:

- **Issue A**: this is the loan-arithmetic signature exactly — a multi-step calculation (NOPAT → invested capital → ratio) where an intermediate value gets correctly computed but then lost or drifted by the time it's used again downstream. The content isn't fabricated (NOPAT was calculated correctly the first time), and nothing suggests the task exceeds the model's capability — it's a structural tracking problem across steps, which chain-of-thought-style scaffolding (explicitly showing and re-referencing intermediate values at each step) directly addresses, exactly as it did in your original loan scenario.

- **Issue B**: this is a clean hallucination case, and worth noting it's a slightly different flavor from your legal-citation scenario — there, the surrounding content was accurate and only a supporting citation was fabricated; here, the *entire central claim* is fabricated (supply chain disruption isn't mentioned anywhere in the actual document, while the real top risks — regulatory and currency exposure — are ignored entirely). Same underlying mechanism though: a confident claim that traces to nothing in the actual provided source. This is important to catch because it happened with a document *fully provided* to the model (unlike your pricing scenario, where grounding was entirely absent) — showing that hallucination can occur even with source material present, if the model doesn't reliably tie its claims back to what's actually there. The fix is a groundedness safeguard — requiring or verifying that summary claims are traceable to specific passages in the source, not just trusting the model read the whole document faithfully.

- **Issue C**: this is where the scenario does something new — it explicitly confirms, as a stated fact rather than something to guess at, that grounding was verified adequate ("full, correctly grounded financial statements") and prompting was verified adequate ("well-structured, chain-of-thought-guided prompt... explicitly asking it to reason through each line item's relationships step by step"). With both of the cheaper, more common causes explicitly ruled out, and the failure specifically clustering on the genuinely hardest cases (subtle, multi-line-item patterns) while succeeding on easier ones (obvious, single-line anomalies) — that's precisely the model-mismatch signature: "failures that persist even with proper grounding and well-constructed prompting, specifically clustering on the genuinely hardest subset of cases." This is a legitimate case for evaluating a more capable model tier, following the same discipline as your Domain 2 coding-assistant and intake-triage scenarios — the reasoning depth requirement (forensic-accountant-level pattern recognition across interacting line items) was established as real and specific, and cheaper explanations were exhausted first, not skipped.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** collapses three genuinely distinct root causes into one, and proposes a fix ("more explicit instructions") that's actively irrelevant to two of the three issues — no amount of added instruction text fixes a fabricated claim about document content (Issue B) or a genuine capability ceiling on subtle multi-line-item reasoning (Issue C); more prompt detail is only really the right lever for Issue A.
- **C)** repeats the mistake from your last scenario's wrong answer D — labeling structural and capability issues as hallucination just because the output is wrong in some sense. Issue A isn't fabricating anything (its intermediate values are real, just mishandled), and Issue C isn't fabricating anything either (the model isn't inventing false patterns, it's failing to detect real subtle ones) — "hallucination" specifically means confident fabrication of unsupported content, not any wrong answer generally.
- **D)** gets the two easier categories backwards by defaulting to model upgrades for A and B — exactly the mistake ruled out by the diagnostic ordering (cheaper causes first) — while dismissing C as unsolvable, when C is actually the one case in this whole set where the evidence *does* support considering a capability upgrade, precisely because the cheaper causes were already checked and ruled out. This inverts the entire discipline: reaching for the expensive fix where it isn't warranted, and giving up where it actually is.

**Pattern to remember, completing this diagnostic arc**: this scenario shows what a *legitimate* model-mismatch case actually requires as evidence — not just "the model got it wrong on a hard task," but specifically "grounding was confirmed adequate, prompting was confirmed well-constructed (including chain-of-thought where relevant), and the failure still persists specifically on the genuinely hardest subset of cases while succeeding on easier ones." Every other scenario across this entire session that tempted you with a model-upgrade answer was missing at least one of these three confirmations — this is the first one where all three are explicitly present, which is exactly what makes it the correct case to conclude model mismatch rather than another trap.
