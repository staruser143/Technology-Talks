**Scenario**

A healthcare software company builds a Claude-powered clinical documentation assistant that converts doctors' dictated notes into structured medical records. To evaluate the system before launch, the team builds an eval set of 500 dictated notes randomly sampled from six months of historical recordings. Running their evaluation, the system scores 94% accuracy — a strong result — and the team prepares to launch.

A clinical advisor reviewing the eval set composition raises a concern: of the 500 randomly sampled notes, only 6 involve dictation with background noise or overlapping speech (common in busy ER settings), only 3 involve non-native English speaker accents, and none involve the kind of rushed, fragmented dictation style doctors use during genuine emergencies — even though the advisor knows from experience that these three categories, combined, represent a meaningful and clinically important share of real-world usage, particularly in exactly the high-stakes situations where documentation accuracy matters most.

**Question**: What's the core issue with this eval set, and what should the team do?

A) There's no issue — random sampling from six months of real historical recordings is a statistically sound, unbiased method, and a 94% score based on it is trustworthy regardless of how the underlying cases happen to be distributed.

B) The core issue is that random sampling produced an eval set that mirrors the natural frequency of these harder cases in historical data, but a natural-frequency sample under-represents exactly the difficult, high-stakes categories (noisy/overlapping speech, accented speech, emergency fragmented dictation) where failure matters most and where the system is least likely to have been implicitly tuned to perform well — the same "traffic isn't uniform, and the hard minority matters most" principle from earlier scenarios, now applied to eval set composition. The team should deliberately oversample or specifically construct additional test cases in these underrepresented-but-critical categories, evaluating them with their own dedicated metrics, rather than relying on their natural low frequency in a randomly sampled set to represent them adequately.

C) The issue is that the eval set is too small; simply increasing the random sample from 500 to 5,000 notes would proportionally increase the hard-case count enough to resolve the concern.

D) The clinical advisor's concern is not a data science issue and should be handled separately from the technical evaluation process, since it relates to clinical judgment rather than statistical methodology.

Take your best guess and I'll walk through it.


Correct — and this scenario is the eval-set-design mirror of your fraud-detection metric scenario: same underlying mechanism (a natural, real-world imbalance quietly under-representing the cases that matter most), just relocated from *how you measure* to *what you test against*.

**Why B is right**

The critical insight the clinical advisor raises is precisely the one flagged in the concepts overview: random sampling that mirrors natural frequency is not the same as ensuring adequate representation of what matters most. Six months of historical recordings naturally contain mostly routine, clear dictation — that's simply what most clinical documentation looks like day to day. A random 500-note sample faithfully reflects that natural distribution, which means it faithfully reflects the *scarcity* of noisy, accented, and emergency-fragmented dictation too — 6 out of 500, 3 out of 500, and 0 out of 500 respectively. Those sample sizes are far too small to draw any reliable conclusion about system performance on those specific categories (the "5 out of 200 for a critical edge case" problem named directly in the concepts overview), and the zero-representation category (emergency fragmented dictation) means the eval set says *literally nothing* about how the system performs there — the 94% score has no bearing on it at all.

The stakes compound the problem in exactly the way the scenario is built to highlight: these underrepresented categories aren't just statistically thin, they're clinically described as "exactly the high-stakes situations where documentation accuracy matters most" — noisy ER environments and genuine emergencies are precisely where a documentation error could have real consequences, and precisely where a system is least likely to have organically encountered enough similar training exposure to perform reliably without dedicated evaluation attention. This is the identical structural lesson from your fraud-detection scenario: a system can score very well in aggregate while performing poorly on a small-but-critical slice, and — just as there — the fix isn't to trust that the aggregate score somehow implies acceptable performance everywhere, it's to specifically construct evaluation coverage for the slice that matters, independent of how rare it happens to be in raw historical frequency.

The fix, as B states, is deliberate oversampling or dedicated construction of test cases in these categories — actively seeking out or synthesizing additional noisy, accented, and emergency-style dictation examples so each category has enough cases to draw a real conclusion from, evaluated with metrics specific to those categories, rather than letting their natural rarity mean they're essentially untested despite technically being present in the sample.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** treats "random sampling" as synonymous with "adequate representation of what matters," which is exactly the false equivalence this scenario is built to expose — random sampling is unbiased with respect to *matching the natural distribution*, but that's a completely different property from *ensuring the difficult, high-stakes minority is adequately tested*. The two would only coincide if difficulty and clinical stakes happened to correlate with frequency, which the advisor's concern directly establishes they don't here.
- **C)** proposes brute-force scaling as if it were a substitute for deliberate design — increasing to 5,000 notes while keeping pure random sampling would indeed yield more absolute noisy/accented/emergency cases (roughly 60, 30, and still likely very few or zero, respectively, if emergencies are rare enough in routine historical data), but this is an expensive, imprecise way to fix a problem that deliberate oversampling solves directly and far more cheaply — 10x-ing your entire dataset to indirectly get a few dozen more cases in three specific categories is far less efficient than simply constructing or targeted-sourcing examples in exactly those categories.
- **D)** draws an artificial line between "clinical judgment" and "evaluation methodology" that the scenario doesn't support — the advisor's concern *is* a data science/eval-design issue, expressed through clinical expertise about which cases matter most; domain expertise informing what an eval set should deliberately cover is a normal, necessary part of eval design, not a separate track that can be handled apart from it. Dismissing it as out-of-scope would mean the technical evaluation process proceeds blind to exactly the input needed to make it actually adequate.

**Pattern to remember**: this scenario and your fraud-detection metric scenario are the same lesson from two different layers of the evaluation pipeline — there, the *metric* (overall accuracy) let majority-class performance mask minority-class failure; here, the *eval set composition* (random natural-frequency sampling) lets majority-case volume crowd out adequate coverage of the minority cases that matter most. Both failures are invisible from the aggregate number alone, and both require the same corrective instinct: deliberately identify what matters most, and make sure it's specifically and adequately represented — never assume natural frequency or a technically-unbiased sampling method automatically produces adequate coverage of what's rare but consequential.
