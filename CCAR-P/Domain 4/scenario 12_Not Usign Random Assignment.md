**Scenario**

A B2B software company wants to test a new Claude-powered onboarding-guidance chatbot (variant) against their current one (control). To implement the test quickly, an engineer sets it up so that: any user whose company account was created before a certain date sees the control, and any user whose account was created after that date sees the variant — new signups get routed to the variant automatically as they come in, rather than being randomly assigned. After a properly long duration and large sample size, the variant shows meaningfully higher onboarding completion rates than the control, and the team concludes the new chatbot is genuinely better and plans to roll it out fully.

Before finalizing, a data analyst points out a concern: because signup date determines assignment rather than random allocation, the variant group consists entirely of *more recent* customers, while the control group consists entirely of *older* customers — and the company has also been running new-customer discount promotions, improving their sales qualification process, and seeing generally different (often more tech-forward) customer profiles sign up in the more recent period, completely independent of which chatbot version anyone saw.

**Question**: What's the core problem with this test design, and what should the team have done differently?

A) There's no problem — the sample size and duration were adequate, and adequate sample size and duration are the only requirements for a trustworthy A/B test result.

B) The core problem is that assignment wasn't random — group membership (control vs. variant) is confounded with signup date, and signup date is correlated with other factors (discount promotions, improved sales qualification, different customer profiles) that independently affect onboarding completion, regardless of which chatbot a user saw. This means the observed difference in completion rates could be entirely or partly explained by these confounding factors rather than the chatbot change itself — the test cannot cleanly attribute the result to the variant being better. The team should have used random assignment (e.g., randomly splitting users regardless of signup date) so that both arms are statistically comparable on every dimension except the change being tested, isolating the chatbot's actual effect from unrelated trends occurring over the same period.

C) The issue is that the test ran for too long; a shorter test duration would have prevented the customer-profile changes from affecting the results.

D) The issue is real but unfixable after the fact; the team has no way to determine whether the variant is actually better and must abandon evaluating this chatbot change entirely.

Correct — and this scenario surfaces a distinct A/B testing failure mode from everything else in this arc: not insufficient volume (your first scenario), not an incomplete metric set (your second), not bundled changes destroying attribution (your third) — this one is about **assignment itself not being random**, which undermines the test's basic premise before you even get to measuring anything.

**Why B is right**

The whole logic of an A/B test rests on one core guarantee: if assignment to control vs. variant is random, then on average, everything *else* about the two groups should be comparable — same mix of customer types, same exposure to unrelated business changes, same everything except the one thing you're testing. That guarantee is what lets you attribute a difference in outcomes to the change itself, rather than to some other difference between the groups.

This test breaks that guarantee at the design level: assignment is based on **signup date**, not randomization — meaning group membership is perfectly correlated with *time*, and time, as the analyst points out, is also correlated with several other things happening independently: discount promotions, an improved sales qualification process, and a shift toward more tech-forward customer profiles. Any or all of these could plausibly improve onboarding completion on their own, with zero contribution from the chatbot at all. A more qualified, more incentivized, more tech-savvy recent cohort might simply complete onboarding at a higher rate regardless of which chatbot version greets them — and because the *test design* has fully entangled "saw the variant" with "signed up recently, under different conditions," there's no way to separate the chatbot's real effect from these confounds using this data. The variant might be genuinely better, might be no better at all, or might even be worse while still showing a positive result purely because it happened to be shown to an inherently easier-to-onboard population.

The fix, correctly stated: **random assignment**, independent of signup date or any other time-correlated factor — split users randomly regardless of when they signed up, so that both the control and variant groups draw from the *same* mix of customer profiles, promotional exposure, and sales-process conditions during the *same* time window. That's what would let a difference in outcomes actually be attributed to the chatbot change, rather than to whatever else happened to be shifting over the same period.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** conflates two genuinely separate requirements for a trustworthy test — adequate sample size/duration (which this scenario has) tells you whether you have enough data to detect a real effect reliably; random assignment tells you whether the effect you're detecting is actually caused by what you think it's caused by. This scenario has the first property and lacks the second, and no amount of additional volume or duration fixes a confounded assignment — you'd just be measuring the confound more precisely, not eliminating it.
- **C)** misdiagnoses the mechanism — shortening the test duration doesn't address the confound at all, since the confound (signup-date-based assignment) exists structurally regardless of how long the test runs; if anything, a shorter test might simply capture a narrower slice of the same time-correlated confound rather than removing it, and it would also reduce the statistical power the test otherwise had.
- **D)** overcorrects into treating a design flaw as unfixable and giving up entirely — but the fix here (switch to random assignment) is straightforward and well within reach; the team doesn't need to abandon evaluating the chatbot, they need to *re-run the test correctly* with proper randomization. Declaring the whole effort unsalvageable discards a legitimate, answerable question over a design mistake that has a clear, standard correction.

**Pattern to remember, adding the fourth distinct A/B testing failure mode to this arc**: you've now seen four genuinely different ways an A/B test can mislead — too little data to distinguish signal from noise (scenario 1), measuring only the metric you hoped to improve while missing a hidden cost (scenario 2), bundling changes so you can't attribute the result to any one of them (scenario 3), and now, assignment that isn't actually random, confounding the thing you're testing with unrelated factors that happen to correlate with group membership (this scenario). All four can produce a test that looks statistically clean and confident on its face — the flaw in each case lives in the *design*, not in whether the resulting number is technically well-computed, which is exactly why "is this test rigorous" requires checking more than just "was the math done correctly."
