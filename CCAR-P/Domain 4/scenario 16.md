**Scenario**

A company's Claude-powered customer support system launched 14 months ago with a well-optimized setup: a lean system prompt, appropriate model tier for the task, and prompt caching correctly configured — validated at launch with token counting and cost projections that matched actual spend closely for the first several months. Nobody has revisited the cost/token profile since, because "it was optimized properly at launch and nothing dramatic happened."

Over the 14 months since, the system prompt has grown from 800 tokens to roughly 2,400 tokens — through numerous small, individually reasonable additions (a new guardrail after an incident, a clarifying instruction after a support-team request, a few-shot example added to fix a formatting complaint) — and a finance review this quarter shows per-conversation cost has risen 65% compared to launch, with no corresponding increase in conversation volume or complexity to explain it. No single change was ever large enough to trigger anyone's attention at the time it was made.

**Question**: What's the core issue here, and what does it imply about how cost/token optimization should be treated as an ongoing discipline?

A) There's no issue — since the system was correctly optimized at launch and no single change was individually alarming, the current cost level simply reflects the system's natural evolution and requires no further action.

B) The core issue is treating token/cost optimization as a one-time, launch-only decision rather than an ongoing discipline requiring periodic re-verification — small, individually reasonable additions accumulated over 14 months into a 3x larger system prompt and a 65% cost increase that nobody caught in real time, because no single addition was large enough to trigger scrutiny and nobody was periodically re-measuring the aggregate. This mirrors the same accumulation pattern as guardrail bloat and capability bloat, just measured in tokens/cost — the fix is periodic token/cost audits (not just a launch-time check), with each new addition evaluated against its actual necessity, similar to how guardrails and tools should be periodically audited for continued relevance.

C) The issue is that prompt caching must have stopped working correctly, since costs rose despite caching being configured; the team should focus exclusively on debugging the caching mechanism.

D) The issue is that the model was upgraded to a more expensive tier at some point without anyone noticing, and the team should audit for an unauthorized model change specifically.

Correct — and this scenario closes the loop precisely on the point the deep dive was making: this is the exact same accumulation pattern from your guardrail-bloat and capability-bloat scenarios, just relocated to token cost specifically, which is exactly why this objective earns its own place in Domain 4 rather than being fully redundant with Domains 2 and 3.

**Why B is right**

Trace the mechanism, since it's now a very familiar shape: each addition — a guardrail after an incident, a clarifying instruction after a support request, a few-shot example to fix a formatting complaint — was individually reasonable and individually small enough that nobody would have flagged it in isolation. That's precisely how the retail agent's tool bloat happened, and precisely how the 40-guardrail customer support assistant's instruction bloat happened. The mechanism here is identical; only the unit of measurement changed from "tools" or "instructions" to "tokens and dollars." A system prompt tripling in size (800 → 2,400 tokens) over 14 months, and per-conversation cost rising 65% with no offsetting increase in volume or complexity to explain it, is the token-cost signature of exactly the same "nobody revisited the aggregate" failure.

The critical detail worth naming precisely: the launch-time validation (token counting, cost projections matching actual spend) was genuinely done well — this isn't a case of sloppy initial engineering. The failure is entirely in treating that validation as a one-time gate rather than an ongoing check, the same "A/B test result treated as permanently settled" mistake from your travel-booking scenario, just applied to cost optimization instead of a production decision's continued validity. Fourteen months of "nothing dramatic happened" masked a genuinely dramatic cumulative outcome, because dramatic-in-aggregate and dramatic-at-any-single-moment are different things — exactly the gap that let the guardrail and capability-bloat scenarios' problems go unnoticed too.

The fix follows directly: periodic token/cost audits (not just at launch), where each addition is evaluated against actual necessity at the time of proposal, *and* the aggregate is periodically re-measured against what it's actually costing now versus what it costs to fulfill the same task's genuine needs — echoing the same evidence-based audit discipline (measure actual usage/cost against configured capability, not against how each piece felt justified when added) you've now applied to tools, guardrails, and, here, tokens.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** repeats the exact "no single change was alarming, so nothing needs attention" complacency the scenario is built to expose — but that's precisely the mechanism by which accumulation problems evade detection: no individual step trips a threshold, yet the sum is a real, measurable 65% cost increase. "Correctly optimized at launch" describes a starting condition, not a permanent guarantee, especially once 14 months of additive, uncoordinated changes have occurred since.
- **C)** reaches for a plausible-sounding technical explanation (caching broken) without evidence supporting it, and misses that caching working *correctly* is actually fully consistent with this scenario — caching reduces the cost of reprocessing a *given* prefix, but it doesn't prevent that prefix from growing in the first place; a 2,400-token system prompt being cached efficiently still costs more per cache-write and more per generated response than an 800-token one would, even with caching functioning exactly as designed. The problem isn't a caching malfunction, it's that there's simply more content to cache and process than there used to be.
- **D)** invents an unsupported explanation (an unauthorized model change) with nothing in the scenario pointing to it — the scenario explicitly attributes the cost increase to system prompt growth through incremental additions, a much more directly evidenced and far more common real-world cause than a silent, undetected model tier change, which would typically be a much more visible, deliberate action (and one the finance review would likely have specifically flagged as a line-item change, distinct from a gradual per-conversation cost creep).

Once you recognize the shape, the diagnostic and the fix transfer directly, regardless of whether what's accumulating is tools, guardrail instructions, or plain token count.
