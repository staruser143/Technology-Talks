**Scenario**

A logistics company engages an architect to build a Claude-powered "shipment exception" system. Reviewing the full project retrospective six months post-launch surfaces the following:

- **Discovery**: The initial stakeholder (VP of Logistics) asked for "an AI that automatically resolves all shipment exceptions without human involvement, to cut our exception-handling costs." The architect proceeded directly to designing a fully autonomous system based on this request, without exploring what specific problems were driving exception costs, without discovering that a separate compliance stakeholder had an unstated requirement that any exception involving customs/international shipments legally required a named human reviewer, and without talking to the exception-handling staff who would have flagged that most cost was coming from a narrow, specific category of easily-automatable domestic exceptions — not the harder international cases the VP's autonomous vision would have prioritized building for.

- **Communicating trade-offs**: When the compliance conflict was eventually discovered mid-build (costing weeks of rework), the architect explained the necessary redesign to the VP using dense internal engineering terminology about "escalation-based orchestration with confidence-gated human-in-the-loop routing," leaving the VP unable to actually evaluate whether the revised approach still met her cost-reduction goals.

- **Feedback loops/SLA**: Under pressure during contract negotiation, the architect agreed to a "99% automatic resolution rate" SLA before any evaluation had been run, based on general optimism rather than actual data. Monthly stakeholder review meetings since launch have surfaced the same recurring complaint (the system's handling of a specific shipment-type edge case) in five consecutive meetings, each time met with "noted, we'll look into it," with no resolution or explanation ever provided.

- **Documentation**: The delivered documentation describes the final architecture's components but doesn't explain why the human-in-the-loop routing threshold was set at its specific confidence level — a decision that was actually a direct response to the compliance requirement discovered mid-project, not an arbitrary technical choice.

- **Lifecycle/handoff**: Upon completion, the architect sent the documentation link to the operations team and moved to a new client the same week, with no walkthrough or transition period.

**Question**: Which of the following best identifies the full set of distinct Domain 6 issues present, each correctly mapped to its sub-topic?

A) There is one root problem — poor initial discovery — and if discovery had been done correctly at the start, none of the other four issues would have occurred, since they're all downstream consequences of the same original mistake.

B) Five distinct, independently-diagnosable issues, each requiring its own fix regardless of the others: (1) discovery — the architect accepted the VP's stated solution without probing the underlying cost driver, missed a conflicting compliance requirement, and never consulted the actual operational staff, mirroring the "stated solution vs. actual problem" and "unreconciled stakeholder conflict" failures from earlier scenarios; (2) trade-off communication — explaining the necessary redesign in dense jargon left the VP unable to evaluate whether it still served her actual goal, the same failure as presenting a technically-accurate-but-unusable explanation; (3) feedback loops/SLA — committing to an unvalidated 99% figure under negotiation pressure, and running five consecutive stakeholder meetings where the same complaint resurfaces with zero resolution or acknowledgment, are two separate instances of the same underlying discipline (honest, closed-loop stakeholder communication) failing in two different ways; (4) documentation — capturing what the routing threshold is without capturing why it exists (a direct compliance-driven decision), leaving future maintainers unable to understand its significance; (5) lifecycle/handoff — treating document delivery as equivalent to a complete handoff, with no transition period or channel for the operations team's inevitable real-world questions. Fixing discovery earlier would have prevented some downstream pain, but the other four failures are independent process gaps that could recur even with perfect discovery, and each needs its own remedy.

C) The only real issue is the unvalidated SLA commitment, since it has the most direct, measurable business consequence; the other four are soft process concerns that don't require specific remediation.

D) All five issues are actually the same underlying problem — poor communication skills on the architect's part — and the fix is generically "communicate better," without needing to address each sub-topic's distinct mechanism separately.


Correct — and this is a fitting capstone for Domain 6, mirroring the compound-scenario treatment you've now applied to every domain in this session: a real engagement accumulating multiple, genuinely independent process failures, each traceable to its own specific sub-topic and its own specific fix.

**Why B is right — confirming each mapping**

1. **Discovery**: this compounds two distinct discovery failures from your earlier scenarios into one instance. The architect took the VP's stated solution ("autonomous, no human involvement") at face value without probing the actual cost driver — directly your chatbot-VP scenario, where "build us a chatbot" needed unpacking before design began. And the architect never surfaced the compliance stakeholder's conflicting requirement or consulted the operational staff who held the actual relevant information (domestic exceptions, not international ones, were the real cost driver) — directly your manufacturing quality-inspection scenario, where talking to only the most senior or vocal stakeholder missed a hard regulatory constraint and the frontline reality entirely.

2. **Trade-off communication**: this is your Response-A failure from the autonomy-vs-workflow scenario, replayed with real consequences — "escalation-based orchestration with confidence-gated human-in-the-loop routing" is accurate, but it doesn't translate to what the VP actually needs to evaluate: does this still cut costs, and by how much. Precision without translation left her unable to meaningfully weigh in on a decision that materially affected her stated goal.

3. **Feedback loops/SLA — two separate failures, correctly kept distinct**: the 99% commitment under pressure is your false-precision scenario exactly — a number manufactured to satisfy negotiation pressure rather than grounded in actual evaluation, setting up a foreseeable credibility problem. The five-consecutive-meetings pattern is your feedback-*meeting*-vs-feedback-*loop* scenario exactly — recurring input with zero closure, whether through resolution or honest acknowledgment of non-prioritization. These are genuinely separate mechanisms (one is about the honesty of an upfront commitment, the other is about whether an ongoing channel actually closes its loop) that happen to share the same root discipline.

4. **Documentation**: capturing *what* the threshold is without *why* it exists is your calculation-drift-verification scenario precisely — a decision that looks arbitrary or adjustable to a future reader is actually load-bearing (here, a compliance requirement), and without documented rationale, a future engineer has no way to know that changing it risks reintroducing a legal/regulatory problem rather than just tuning a technical parameter.

5. **Lifecycle/handoff**: document-delivery-as-handoff, with an immediate departure and no transition window, is your fraud-review-system scenario exactly — even reasonably good documentation doesn't substitute for a structured transition period where the receiving team can ask the questions that only surface once real operational experience begins.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** correctly identifies discovery as a real, contributing failure, but overstates its explanatory reach — better discovery would likely have prevented the compliance conflict and some of the mid-project rework, but it has no bearing on whether the SLA commitment was validated, whether the redesign was explained in accessible terms, whether recurring meeting complaints ever got closed out, whether documentation captured rationale, or whether handoff included a transition period. These are all failures that could occur, and often do, even in projects with excellent discovery — treating them as pure downstream consequences of one root cause repeats the single-root-cause mistake rejected in every compound scenario across this entire session.
- **C)** arbitrarily elevates the SLA issue for having the most visible business consequence while dismissing four other issues with real, evidenced costs — weeks of mid-project rework from the missed compliance conflict, a VP unable to actually evaluate a redesign affecting her stated goals, five months of stakeholder frustration with zero acknowledgment, a future maintainer risk from undocumented compliance rationale, and an operations team left guessing inconsistently on real cases. None of these are "soft" in the sense of lacking real consequence; they're simply harder to reduce to one clean number than the SLA figure is.
- **D)** collapses five genuinely distinct process mechanisms (probing stated requirements, translating technical trade-offs, honest commitment-setting, closing feedback loops, capturing rationale, structuring transitions) into one vague diagnosis ("communicate better") that offers no actionable path to fixing any of them specifically — this is the Domain 6 equivalent of prescribing "write better code" for five architecturally distinct bugs; each failure here has its own specific discipline and specific fix, and treating them as interchangeable symptoms of one generic weakness obscures exactly the diagnostic precision this domain is testing.

**The meta-lesson closing Domain 6, consistent with every mixed review this session**: a real engagement can accumulate multiple, genuinely independent process failures across discovery, communication, expectation-setting, documentation, and lifecycle management simultaneously — and professional-level competence means correctly enumerating and separately addressing each one, rather than collapsing them into a single narrative or hoping that fixing the most visible failure resolves the rest.
