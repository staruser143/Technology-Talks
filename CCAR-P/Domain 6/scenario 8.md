**Scenario**

A company's Claude-powered internal analytics assistant has been in production for a year, serving several business teams. The project team holds a monthly "stakeholder review" meeting where they present a dashboard of usage stats and recent feature additions to representatives from each business team. The meetings are well-attended, stakeholders occasionally ask questions, and the project team always says "great feedback, we'll take that into consideration."

Reviewing the last 12 months of these meeting notes, a new team lead who's joined the project notices something: stakeholders have raised the same three specific requests (a way to save frequently-used queries, better handling of a particular data format the marketing team uses constantly, and clearer error messages when a request fails) in nearly every single monthly meeting for the entire year — and none of the three has been addressed, discussed as a prioritization trade-off, or even formally acknowledged as "we heard this, here's why it's not prioritized yet." The meetings continue on schedule, stakeholders continue attending, but the same requests keep resurfacing because nothing about them ever gets resolved or explained.

**Question**: What's the core issue with this feedback process, and what does it reveal about the difference between having a feedback *meeting* and having a genuine feedback *loop*?


**Question**: What's the core issue here?

A) There's no real issue — the meetings are well-attended and stakeholders have a regular opportunity to voice concerns, which is the definition of a functioning feedback process; the fact that specific requests haven't been implemented simply reflects reasonable prioritization against other work.

B) The core issue is that this is a feedback **meeting**, not a feedback **loop** — a loop requires a closed cycle: input is received, and something demonstrably happens as a result (implementation, a documented prioritization decision, or at minimum an explicit acknowledgment and explanation of why it's not being addressed). Here, input flows in every month, but nothing measurable flows back out — no resolution, no trade-off explanation, not even a formal "we've heard this, here's our reasoning for deprioritizing it." A stakeholder raising the same issue for 12 consecutive months with zero acknowledgment beyond "great feedback" isn't being heard in any functional sense — they're participating in a process that has the form of feedback collection without the substance of it, and this pattern is exactly what erodes stakeholder trust and eventually engagement over time, even if attendance hasn't dropped yet.

C) The issue is that the three specific requests are simply not important enough to warrant addressing, and stakeholders should stop raising them if the team has implicitly signaled through inaction that they're lower priority.

D) The issue is unrelated to the feedback process itself; the team should focus on fixing the three specific requests immediately, since the actual technical backlog is the only real problem, and the meeting structure itself requires no changes.


Correct — and this scenario names something distinct from the SLA-negotiation scenario, worth being precise about the difference: that one was about setting an honest commitment upfront; this one is about whether an *ongoing* mechanism for stakeholder input is actually functioning as a loop, or just going through the motions of one.

**Why B is right**

The critical diagnostic detail is right there in the scenario: the same three requests, raised in nearly every single monthly meeting for a full year, with zero resolution *and* zero explicit explanation of why they haven't been resolved. That second part matters as much as the first — a genuine feedback loop doesn't require that every request gets implemented; reasonable prioritization against competing work is completely normal and expected. What makes this a broken loop rather than sound prioritization is that **nothing closes the cycle**: no one ever tells these stakeholders "we heard this, here's why it's below our current priorities, here's roughly when or whether it might get addressed." Instead, the response every month is the same content-free acknowledgment ("great feedback, we'll take that into consideration") with no follow-through of any kind — not action, not even an honest explanation of inaction.

This is the structural difference between a feedback *meeting* (a recurring event where people are invited to speak) and a feedback *loop* (a cycle where input demonstrably affects something — either the system itself, or at minimum the stakeholder's understanding of why it hasn't changed yet). A meeting can run indefinitely, well-attended, without ever closing that loop — which is exactly what's happening here, and it's a genuinely different failure from setting an unrealistic SLA; this is about an *ongoing mechanism* quietly hollowing out over time rather than a single upfront commitment being miscalibrated. Left unaddressed, this is exactly the kind of pattern that erodes stakeholder trust and eventually genuine engagement — even before attendance visibly drops, stakeholders likely start viewing the meeting as a formality rather than a real channel, which is a much harder problem to reverse once it sets in.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** treats the existence of a recurring meeting with attendance as sufficient evidence the feedback process works — but attendance and participation are inputs to a feedback loop, not evidence the loop is closing. The scenario specifically shows the *output* side (resolution or acknowledgment) is entirely missing, which is the actual defining feature of whether the process functions, not whether people show up and talk.
- **C)** assumes silence and inaction constitute an implicit "no" the stakeholders should infer and accept — but this is precisely the failure mode B identifies: if the team wants to deprioritize these requests, that's a legitimate call to make, but it needs to be made *explicitly*, with reasoning, not left for stakeholders to guess at through a year of unexplained non-response. Expecting stakeholders to interpret silence as a decision is not the same as actually communicating one.
- **D)** correctly identifies that the underlying requests need real attention, but incorrectly concludes the meeting *structure* itself needs no change — fixing the three specific requests without addressing the structural gap (no mechanism for closing the loop on future requests) would just mean the next set of recurring, unresolved requests eventually replaces these three, since the actual process flaw (input without output) hasn't been fixed, only its current symptom has.

**Pattern to remember, closing this feedback-loop arc**: a functioning stakeholder feedback loop requires more than a regular opportunity to speak — it requires the cycle to actually close, either through action or through explicit, reasoned acknowledgment of inaction. A recurring meeting with attendance and participation can still be a completely broken feedback mechanism if nothing measurable ever comes back out the other side, and the telltale sign — as in this scenario — is the same unresolved requests resurfacing indefinitely with no explanation ever offered for why.
