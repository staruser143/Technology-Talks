**Scenario**

A hospital administrator approaches an architect wanting a Claude-powered system to "handle patient triage decisions automatically, so we can reduce nursing staff time spent on initial patient assessment." During discovery, the administrator states her expectation plainly: "I want this system to make the triage call directly — patient comes in, system decides urgency level, nurse just executes based on what it says. That's the whole point — freeing up nursing time by removing them from the initial decision."

The architect recognizes two things: first, this expectation assumes a level of autonomous decision-making authority that — given what you've already learned about human-in-the-loop design for high-stakes healthcare decisions — is very unlikely to be an appropriate or safe design regardless of the system's technical capability; second, the administrator seems to be treating "AI makes the call, human just executes" as the *only* way to achieve her actual underlying goal (freeing up nursing time).

**Question**: What should the architect do at this stage of discovery?


**Question**: What should the architect do at this stage of discovery?

A) Agree to build the system as described, since the administrator is the client and has clearly articulated her requirement; raising concerns about the safety of full autonomy at this stage could jeopardize the engagement, and it can always be adjusted later if problems arise after launch.

B) Address the expectation mismatch directly and early, as part of discovery itself: explain, using the same reasoning already established for high-stakes healthcare decisions, why full autonomous triage without human review is unlikely to be an appropriate design (irreversible/high-stakes consequences, the kind of decision that warrants at least an escalation-based or pre-action HITL model rather than full automation) — while also clarifying that this doesn't mean her actual goal (freeing up nursing time) is unachievable; a well-designed system with appropriate human oversight (e.g., the AI handling initial assessment and confidently routing clear-cut cases, escalating ambiguous or high-risk cases for nurse review) can still meaningfully reduce nursing time on straightforward cases without removing human judgment from the loop entirely. Surfacing this now, before design commitments are made, prevents building toward an unsafe or unrealistic expectation and redirects toward the achievable version of her actual goal.

C) Decline the engagement entirely, since any request involving healthcare triage decisions is inherently too risky for AI involvement at any level, regardless of how the human-oversight design might be structured.

D) Proceed with discovery on other aspects of the project without addressing this specific point, planning to raise the autonomy concern later during the architecture design phase instead, since discovery should focus only on gathering requirements, not evaluating whether those requirements are appropriate.


Correct — and this scenario introduces a discovery skill distinct from your first two: not just surfacing the *real problem* behind a stated solution, or reconciling *conflicting* stakeholder requirements, but recognizing when a stakeholder's stated requirement itself reflects a **misunderstanding of what's appropriate or achievable**, and correcting that early rather than deferring the conversation.

**Why B is right**

The administrator has bundled two things together as if they were one: her actual goal (free up nursing time spent on initial assessment) and a specific implementation she believes is the only way to get there (full autonomous decision-making, human just executes). The architect's job in discovery isn't just to record this as a requirement and move on — it's to recognize that the *implementation* she's describing conflicts with everything you've already established about appropriate human-in-the-loop design for high-stakes, hard-to-reverse healthcare decisions (directly echoing your loan-scheduling, salary-change, and prior-authorization material from earlier domains). A wrong triage call is exactly the kind of consequential, potentially irreversible outcome that warrants a human checkpoint, not full automation — regardless of how technically capable the underlying model is.

What makes B the right response, rather than just "raise a safety objection," is that it does two things together: it names the concern using established, defensible reasoning (not just "this feels risky" but the specific stakes/reversibility logic from your HITL material), *and* it reframes toward the achievable version of her actual underlying goal. This is the crucial move — the architect isn't telling the administrator "no, this can't be done," she's clarifying that the *specific implementation* she proposed isn't appropriate, while showing that her real objective (reduced nursing time) is still very achievable through a better-designed approach: escalation-based HITL, where the system confidently handles clear-cut cases and routes ambiguous or high-risk ones to a nurse, still meaningfully reducing time spent on the straightforward majority without removing human judgment from where it matters most. This is literally the Workflow-3-style escalation-based reasoning from your very first HITL scenario, now being explained to a stakeholder rather than applied technically.

Doing this *during discovery*, before any design commitment, is what makes it valuable — surfacing this concern now costs a conversation; surfacing it after a full-autonomy system has been designed, built, or even partially deployed costs a redesign, wasted effort, and potentially real patient-safety exposure in the interim.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** defers a serious, foreseeable safety concern to avoid an uncomfortable conversation, and rationalizes this with "it can be adjusted later" — but adjusting *after* a full-autonomy system has been designed and possibly deployed is far more costly and risky than addressing the mismatch now, and "avoid jeopardizing the engagement" is prioritizing the relationship over the actual safety of the system being proposed, which is precisely backwards for an architect's professional responsibility.
- **C)** overcorrects into refusing any AI involvement in triage whatsoever, which isn't supported by anything in this domain's material — you've established repeatedly that appropriately-scoped human oversight (escalation-based, pre-action approval for high-stakes portions) is the right tool for high-stakes decisions, not "AI should never touch this at all." Declining the entire engagement discards a legitimate, achievable version of the administrator's actual goal over a concern that has a real, workable design solution.
- **D)** treats discovery as a pure requirement-gathering exercise disconnected from judgment about whether those requirements are sound — but this misunderstands the architect's role entirely; discovery isn't just transcription of stakeholder wishes, it includes the professional responsibility to flag when a stated requirement reflects a misunderstanding of what's safe or achievable, and doing so as early as possible (during discovery, not later during design) is precisely what prevents costly, unsafe design commitments from being made in the first place.

**Pattern to remember, completing this discovery arc**: this scenario, alongside your first two, shows discovery operating on three distinct axes — surfacing the real problem behind a stated solution, reconciling conflicts *between* stakeholders, and now, correcting a mismatch *within* a single stakeholder's own request (between their stated implementation and their actual underlying goal, when the implementation itself is inappropriate). All three share the same underlying discipline: don't accept a stakeholder's first framing at face value and proceed straight to design — dig into what's actually needed, what's actually achievable, and what's actually safe, and have that conversation while it's still cheap to have it.
