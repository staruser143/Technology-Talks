**Scenario**

An insurance company uses Claude to review auto-claim submissions and produce a specific output: a structured decision memo with exactly four labeled sections in a fixed order — "Summary of Claim," "Applicable Policy Terms," "Reasoning," and "Recommendation (Approve/Deny/Escalate)" — where the Reasoning section must show the step-by-step logic connecting the claim facts to the policy terms before arriving at the recommendation (this is a compliance requirement — auditors need to see the reasoning chain, not just the final answer).

The current zero-shot prompt just says: "Review this claim and provide a decision memo: {claim details} {policy terms}." Testing reveals two separate problems: (1) the memo's section structure and labeling varies from response to response — sometimes sections are renamed, reordered, or merged, and (2) even when the structure happens to come out right, the "Reasoning" section sometimes jumps straight to a conclusion without actually showing the logical steps connecting specific policy clauses to the specific claim facts, which fails the compliance requirement.

**Question**: Given both problems, what's the most appropriate prompt engineering fix?

A) Chain-of-thought only — since the compliance issue (missing reasoning steps) is the more serious problem, adding "think step by step" instructions alone will resolve both the structure and the reasoning gaps.

B) Few-shot only — since providing example memos in the correct format will fix the structural inconsistency, and seeing the format modeled will also naturally fix the reasoning-depth problem as a side effect.

C) Combine both: use few-shot examples that demonstrate the exact four-section structure AND show a fully worked-out reasoning chain within the Reasoning section of each example, so the examples simultaneously model the required format (fixing problem 1) and demonstrate the expected depth of step-by-step reasoning (fixing problem 2) — since the two problems are distinct failure modes that happen to co-occur in this task, each needing its own targeted technique, most effectively delivered together in the same demonstrated examples.

D) Neither technique is appropriate for a compliance-sensitive task like this; the decision memo should be generated by a fixed template with no LLM involvement in the reasoning at all.

Take your best guess and I'll walk through it.


Correct — and this scenario is designed to show that "combine both techniques" doesn't just mean stapling two separate instructions together — it means recognizing that the *same* few-shot examples can carry both signals at once, because format and reasoning depth aren't actually separate additions, they're both dimensions of what a good example demonstrates.

**Why C is right**

Walk through the two problems and confirm they're genuinely distinct failure modes, the way you've now learned to check for in every combined-pattern scenario:

- **Problem 1 (structure varies)**: sections renamed, reordered, merged — this is the same signature as your product-description scenario. Content isn't necessarily wrong, but there's no consistent, hard-to-fully-specify-in-words structural convention being followed. That's a few-shot signature.
- **Problem 2 (reasoning sometimes skips straight to a conclusion)**: even when the structure is right, the *content of the Reasoning section* sometimes fails to show the actual logical chain connecting policy clauses to claim facts. That's a chain-of-thought signature — the same pattern as your loan-approval arithmetic scenario, where the model needs explicit scaffolding to reliably work through intermediate steps rather than compressing straight to an answer.

Because these are genuinely separate failure modes (you could imagine a memo with perfect structure and shallow reasoning, or a memo with a real reasoning chain in the wrong section labels), fixing only one leaves the other exposed — exactly like your podcast-transcript and workflow-plus-agentic combined-pattern scenarios from Domain 1, where addressing only one part of a multi-part problem left a real gap.

What makes C the precise answer rather than just "yes, use both" is *how* they combine: the few-shot examples themselves are the delivery mechanism for *both* fixes simultaneously. An example memo that has the correct four-section structure **and** a fully worked-out Reasoning section (e.g., "Policy Section 4.2 excludes damage from off-road use → claim states vehicle was driven on a marked trail → trail conditions meet the policy's definition of 'unpaved surface incidental to normal use' → therefore exclusion does not apply → recommend Approve") teaches the model two things at once: what the output shape should look like, and what depth of step-by-step reasoning is expected within it. This is more efficient and more reliable than trying to achieve the same result with separate, disconnected instructions ("use this structure" + "also think step by step") because the demonstrated example makes the *specific* expected reasoning granularity concrete, in exactly the compliance-relevant domain, rather than leaving "step by step" as an abstract instruction the model has to interpret on its own.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** correctly identifies the reasoning gap as serious (it's a compliance requirement, real stakes) but assumes a generic "think step by step" instruction alone would also fix the structural inconsistency — it wouldn't. Nothing about being told to reason step-by-step tells the model which four section labels to use, in which order, or how to format them; that's a separate, format-specific problem that chain-of-thought doesn't address at all, the same gap identified in your product-description scenario's wrong answer A.
- **B)** assumes structural fidelity from examples would "naturally" fix reasoning depth as a side effect — but this only holds if the examples themselves happen to demonstrate deep reasoning, which is exactly what C makes explicit and B leaves as an unstated assumption. If the few-shot examples showed correct section labels but shallow, conclusion-jumping reasoning within the Reasoning section (just correctly labeled shallow reasoning), the model would likely learn to replicate *that* — correct structure, still-inadequate reasoning depth. The fix only works if the examples are deliberately constructed to model both dimensions, which is precisely C's distinction from B.
- **D)** overcorrects into removing Claude from the task entirely for a problem that's actually well-suited to targeted prompting fixes — the scenario doesn't provide evidence that prompting techniques are insufficient here; it shows two specific, diagnosable, fixable failure modes. This repeats the same overcorrection pattern from your capability-bloat and auth-scenario wrong answers: discard a working approach's core value instead of applying the right fix to it. A "fixed template with no LLM reasoning" would also defeat the actual purpose of the task, since claim review requires the kind of nuanced fact-to-policy reasoning that's exactly what Claude is being used for here.

**Pattern to remember, capping this sub-topic**: when a task shows two distinct failure signatures at once (format/consistency issues *and* reasoning-depth issues), the fix isn't necessarily "apply technique A to problem A and technique B to problem B" as separate additions — it's recognizing that well-constructed few-shot examples can be the *vehicle* for demonstrating both the desired structure and the desired reasoning depth simultaneously, since an example is not just a format template, it's also a demonstration of what "good" looks like at every level of the output, including how thoroughly the reasoning inside it should be shown.
