**Scenario**

A mid-sized healthtech company runs a Claude-powered clinician assistant that helps doctors quickly find relevant information during patient visits. The system has grown over 14 months and now includes:

- A RAG pipeline over medical reference documents and internal care protocols, using fixed-size chunking (500 tokens, no overlap) with pure vector search and no metadata filtering — care protocols get revised periodically, and old revisions are kept in the knowledge base for compliance record-keeping.
- 11 connected tools: look up patient records, check drug interaction database, check lab results, schedule follow-up appointments, order prescriptions, message other clinicians, check insurance coverage, submit billing codes, request specialist referrals, update patient care plans, and generate discharge summaries. All 11 tool definitions are loaded into context for every conversation. Usage logs show 3 of these tools (patient records, drug interactions, lab results) account for 89% of all invocations; "submit billing codes" and "check insurance coverage" haven't been called in over 3 months, tied to a billing-integration pilot that was discontinued.
- A single shared service account with read/write access across all patient records for all patients in the system, used for every clinician's queries regardless of which patients that specific clinician is actually treating — logs record only "clinician_assistant_service queried patient record #X," with no capture of which clinician initiated the query.
- A monitoring dashboard tracking uptime (99.9%), latency (average 2.1 seconds), and error rates (near zero) — reviewed by the engineering team during a monthly meeting. There is no alerting configured on any metric.
- The system has a stated clinical requirement: responses during an active patient visit must return within 4 seconds, since doctors are consulting it in real time with the patient present. Current average latency (2.1s) is within this, but a recent internal review found that "drug interaction check" queries specifically average 6.5 seconds when a patient has more than 15 active medications, because the current design always performs a full pairwise interaction check across the patient's entire medication list before returning anything, even when the doctor is only asking about one specific new drug.

**Question**: Which of the following is the MOST complete and accurate diagnosis of the distinct problems present in this system, each correctly matched to its Integration sub-topic?

A) There is one core problem — capability bloat from having 11 tools — and fixing that (removing unused tools) will also resolve the RAG issues, the auth issue, and the latency issue, since all of these stem from the system being generally over-built.

B) Five distinct, independently-diagnosable problems: (1) RAG — no metadata filtering on care protocols risks surfacing superseded/outdated protocol versions since old revisions remain in the corpus; (2) capability bloat — two orphaned tools tied to a discontinued pilot should be retired, and all 11 tool schemas loading into every conversation also compounds context cost; (3) auth/authz — a single shared service account with unrestricted all-patient access and no per-clinician attribution is both a scope/least-privilege gap and an audit-attribution gap; (4) observability — dashboard-only monitoring with no alerting means a real problem could go unnoticed until the monthly review, same gap as the identity-verification scenario; (5) accuracy-latency — the drug interaction tool's full pairwise check for all medications is an unnecessary uniform cost that should be scoped to what the doctor actually asked about, especially since it's blowing the 4-second clinical SLA for exactly the patients (high medication count) where fast answers may matter most.

C) The system is fine overall since average latency (2.1s) is within the 4-second SLA and error rates are near zero; the drug-interaction latency outlier for a minority of patients isn't worth addressing given otherwise strong metrics.

D) The only real problem is the auth/authz gap; the RAG, capability bloat, and latency issues are minor implementation details that don't require architectural attention at the professional-architect level.

Take your best guess and I'll walk through it, or ask me to explain any option first if you'd like.




Correct — and this is exactly the kind of compound scenario the real CCAR-P exam is likely to lean on for its harder Integration questions, since it's the sub-topic with the most surface area (19%) and the most internal cross-references. Let's confirm each diagnosis and why the alternatives fail.

**Why B is right — walking through all five diagnoses**

1. **RAG / metadata filtering**: Fixed-size chunking with no overlap is a separate, real weakness, but the *specifically named* gap that matches your policy-scenario training is metadata filtering — old care-protocol revisions are deliberately retained (legitimate compliance reason, same as your university-policy scenario), but with no status/recency filtering, a semantically-similar-but-superseded protocol could easily outrank the current one for a clinician's query. In a clinical context, this isn't a minor inconvenience — it's the exact "confident, wrong answer that looks legitimate" failure mode you diagnosed earlier, now with real patient-care stakes.

2. **Capability bloat**: Two tools with zero calls in three months, tied to a discontinued pilot — this is the orphaned-tool signature from your retail scenario, verbatim. Note this is diagnosed *independently* of the "11 tools loading into every context" observation — that second part is a *progressive discovery* concern (context cost), not itself proof of bloat. B correctly keeps these as related but distinct: retire the orphaned tools (bloat fix) *and* separately address how much gets loaded per conversation (discovery fix) — echoing the point from two scenarios ago that these are complementary, not substitutable.

3. **Auth/authz — two gaps at once**: A single shared service account with unrestricted access to *all* patients regardless of which clinician is asking is the confused-deputy/scope pattern from your CRM scenario (there, it was cross-tenant CRM data; here it's cross-patient access with no relation to the treating clinician). But note B correctly identifies it *doesn't* automatically follow that a shared service account is wrong by itself (recall the hospital scenario, where a shared account was legitimate) — the actual problem is that this shared account isn't scoped to each clinician's actual patients, *and* separately, there's no attribution captured for who queried what. Both gaps from your two auth scenarios, present together, correctly kept distinct.

4. **Observability — dashboard without alerting**: Uptime/latency/error-rate tracked, reviewed monthly, no alerting — structurally identical to the identity-verification scenario. A real problem (like the drug-interaction latency issue itself, or a future spike) could sit unnoticed for up to a month.

5. **Accuracy-latency**: The drug-interaction tool doing a full pairwise check across the entire medication list regardless of what was actually asked is a uniform-cost design applied to a task that doesn't need uniform treatment — very similar in shape to your fraud-detection scenario's fix (route based on actual complexity/need), just with a different lever: instead of an early-exit *classification*, this calls for scoping the *computation itself* to the actual query (check the new drug against the list, not the full list against itself) rather than always doing the maximal version of the check. Worth flagging as the sharpest finding in the whole scenario: this failure clusters specifically among patients with more medications — plausibly the patients where a fast, correct interaction check matters most clinically. That's the same "the expensive failure lands exactly where you can least afford it" pattern from your fraud-review scenario's wrong-answer A.

**Why the others are wrong**

- **A)** commits the "one fix solves everything" error you've now correctly rejected multiple times (the logistics mixed-review scenario, the fraud-detection scenario). These five problems have five different root causes and five different fixes — removing unused tools does nothing for metadata filtering, service-account scoping, alerting, or the drug-interaction computation design.
- **C)** repeats the exact mistake from the identity-verification scenario: treating strong *aggregate* infrastructure metrics (average latency, overall error rate) as proof of health, while a real, clinically-relevant problem hides in a segment the aggregate metric doesn't surface. "Average latency is fine" says nothing about the patients experiencing 6.5-second delays — precisely the kind of segment-level blindness good observability is supposed to catch, and precisely the trap that scenario was built to warn against.
- **D)** arbitrarily elevates one real problem while dismissing four others as "not architectural," with no principled basis given for that ranking — and it's simply inconsistent with everything you've established across this whole domain: RAG design flaws, capability bloat, observability gaps, and latency-scoping issues have each been treated throughout this whole module as genuine architect-level concerns, each with its own diagnostic method and fix. Nothing about this scenario makes those four suddenly "minor."

**The meta-lesson this mixed review is testing**: production systems accumulate problems across *multiple independent sub-topics simultaneously*, the same way your logistics-scenario mixed review showed for Domain 1. Professional-level competence isn't spotting *one* problem in a system — it's correctly enumerating *all* the distinct problems present, attributing each to the right sub-topic and right fix, and not letting one obvious issue (here, arguably the auth gap, given the clinical stakes) crowd out the others.
